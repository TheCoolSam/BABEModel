To ground the Dynamic-BABE framework in established social science and computational methodologies, we review the literature across five key domains: classical opinion dynamics, cognitive resistance and biased assimilation, social judgment theory, the co-evolutionary dynamics of network trust, and empirical studies on algorithmic platform interventions.

\subsection{Classical Models of Opinion Dynamics}
The computational study of social influence and consensus formation originates with classical models of opinion dynamics, which mathematically formalize how individuals influence one another over time. The foundational model in this tradition is the linear consensus framework proposed by DeGroot \cite{DeGroot1974}. In the DeGroot model, agents update their opinions through a process of weighted averaging, where each agent's next opinion is a linear combination of their own current opinion and those of their neighbors. While elegant, the classical DeGroot model inevitably leads to global consensus in any connected network, failing to explain the persistence of disagreement or polarization observed in real-world societies.

To address this limitation, subsequent scholars introduced the concept of ``bounded confidence.'' In the Hegselmann-Krause model \cite{Hegselmann2002}, agents only influence one another if their opinions are within a pre-defined threshold, representing a cognitive boundary of similarity. Similarly, the Deffuant model \cite{Deffuant2000} explores pairwise interactions under bounded confidence, demonstrating that when the confidence threshold is small, populations fragment into stable, non-communicating subgroups. While bounded confidence models successfully prevent universal consensus, they rely on a binary threshold where outgroup agents are simply ignored. They do not capture active repulsion or cognitive resistance, which are critical for modeling toxic polarization.

\subsection{Cognitive Resistance and Biased Assimilation}
A more psychologically realistic representation of opinion dynamics must account for the cognitive biases that govern how humans process conflicting information. The psychological foundation of cognitive resistance is ``biased assimilation,'' first empirically demonstrated in the seminal study by Lord, Ross, and Lepper \cite{Lord1979}. Their research showed that when individuals are presented with identical, mixed evidence regarding a controversial topic, they selectively process it: they readily accept evidence that confirms their prior beliefs while subjecting opposing evidence to intense, critical scrutiny. Consequently, exposure to balanced information does not lead to convergence; instead, it causes prior attitudes to polarize.

In computational sociology, scholars have sought to formalize biased assimilation within agent-based systems. Dandekar et al. \cite{Dandekar2013} incorporated biased assimilation into a modified DeGroot model, proving that when agents exhibit cognitive bias, the presence of network homophily accelerates polarization, eventually driving opinions to extreme poles. More recently, Chen et al. \cite{Chen2021} developed the Biased Assimilation and Behavioral Entrenchment (BEBA) framework. The BEBA model introduces an entrenchment parameter, $\beta$, which dynamically governs an agent's cognitive resistance. Entrenched agents are highly resistant to change, and when exposed to highly divergent opinions, their influence weights can become negative, driving their opinions further away from their interaction partners. Our work builds directly on the BEBA formulation, extending it by integrating network-level relational trust.

\subsection{Social Judgment Theory and the Backfire Effect}
To understand the precise thresholds at which opinion exposure shifts from constructive assimilation to destructive repulsion, we turn to Social Judgment Theory, developed by Sherif and Hovland \cite{Sherif1961}. Social Judgment Theory conceptualizes an individual's attitude space as a continuum divided into three distinct regions: the \textit{Latitude of Acceptance} (ideas that are agreeable), the \textit{Latitude of Non-Commitment} (ideas that are neutral), and the \textit{Latitude of Rejection} (ideas that are highly objectionable). 

When an individual is exposed to a communication that falls within their Latitude of Acceptance, they assimilate it, shifting their opinion toward the source. If the communication falls within their Latitude of Rejection, however, a contrast effect occurs, wherein the message is perceived as far more extreme than it actually is. This contrast effect triggers attitude repulsion, commonly referred to as the ``backfire effect,'' driving the individual's opinion away from the source. Jager and Amblard \cite{Jager2005} successfully integrated Social Judgment Theory with network methodologies, demonstrating that when repulsion forces are active, network structures can rapidly shift from uniformity to stable bipolarization and subgroup formation. Our model leverages this three-zone classification, mapping the influence weight directly to the latitudes of acceptance, non-commitment, and rejection.

\subsection{The Co-Evolution of Networks and Dyadic Trust}
While early agent-based models treated social network topologies as static backdrops, contemporary research recognizes that opinions and network structures co-evolve. However, most co-evolutionary models focus on ``structural plasticity''---where agents prune disagreeable edges and form new ones (homphily-driven rewiring). In contrast, we explore ``relational co-evolution,'' where the physical network topology remains stable but the qualitative nature of the relationships, mediated by dyadic interpersonal trust, changes dynamically.

interpersonal trust is a critical mediator of social influence. As empirically established in risk and decision research by Slovic \cite{Slovic1993}, human trust is characterized by a fundamental asymmetry: it is ``fragile''---slow to build through repeated positive interactions but incredibly rapid to destroy through a single negative conflict. In social networks, this dynamic implies that agreement slowly strengthens dyadic ties, whereas backfire-inducing exchanges erode trust rapidly. Incorporating dynamic trust introduces a ``buffer effect'' \cite{Slovic1993}: high trust allows friends to navigate occasional ideological disagreements without destroying their relationship. Conversely, when trust collapses, even moderate disagreements can trigger repulsion, leading to relational decay.

\subsection{Algorithmic Interventions and Exposure Experiments}
Our exploration of the Polarization Paradox is highly relevant to contemporary debates regarding algorithmic content moderation on digital platforms. Historically, platforms assumed that exposing users to cross-cutting, diverse viewpoints would naturally mitigate polarization. However, a landmark field experiment by Bail et al. \cite{Bail2018} challenged this assumption. They exposed Twitter users to a bot that retweets opposing political perspectives and found that Democrats and Republicans actually became significantly more polarized after the exposure, empirically demonstrating the real-world salience of the backfire effect.

Subsequent empirical studies have confirmed that algorithmic feed design profoundly shapes political attitudes and user behaviors \cite{Levy2021, Guess2023}. To mitigate the hostile cascades triggered by the backfire effect, platforms have designed ``bridging algorithms'' designed to suppress divisive outgroup content and prioritize consensus-seeking or non-confrontational interactions \cite{Guess2023}. While empirical studies examine the short-term impact of these algorithms on individual attitudes, they lack the capacity to observe long-term, systemic feedback loops. Our agent-based model fills this gap. We demonstrate that by suppressively shielding agents from outgroup views, bridging algorithms prevent the relational friction required to cultivate long-term tolerance, leading to a structural segregation of trust that locks polarization in place.
