This section presents the quantitative findings from our 2$\times$2 factorial simulation experiments, which cross the presence of the platform's \textbf{Bridging Algorithm} (ON vs. OFF) with the presence of the \textbf{Dyadic Trust} co-evolution system (ON vs. OFF). Each of the four resulting experimental conditions---Baseline (Bridge OFF, Trust OFF), Bridge Only (Bridge ON, Trust OFF), Trust Only (Bridge OFF, Trust ON), and the Full Model (Bridge ON, Trust ON)---was simulated for $30$ independent runs of $200$ steps each, initialized using a fixed master seed ($S = 42$) to guarantee strict reproducibility. All statistical comparisons reported below employ the non-parametric Mann-Whitney $U$ test (two-sided) alongside Bonferroni corrections for family-wise error rates, and Cohen's $d$ is computed to quantify standardized effect sizes \citep{Cohen1988}.

\subsection{The Polarization Paradox: Short-Term Monetization vs. Long-Term Alignment}
The first set of analyses examines the direct system-level consequences of deploying a bridging algorithm in the absence of co-evolving interpersonal trust, comparing the \textbf{Baseline} and \textbf{Bridge Only} conditions. Our results reveal a profound and striking tension between platform-level economic preservation and societal-level ideological consensus---a phenomenon we define as the \textbf{Polarization Paradox}. 

\subsubsection{User Retention and Platform Revenue}
In the Baseline condition, where no moderation is present to suppress ideological conflict, the exposure of entrenched agents to opposing views drives extreme psychological friction. The cumulative user churn rate reaches a devastating mean of $39.18\%$ ($\text{SD} = 2.27\%$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [38.39\%, 39.98\%]$) by step $200$. This high level of user flight significantly reduces the active user population from the initial $500$ agents to a final mean of $304.1$ active users ($\text{SD} = 11.35$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [300.10, 308.03]$). 

Concomitantly, the average frustration among the remaining active users escalates to $0.9668$ ($\text{SD} = 0.1669$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.9055, 1.0239]$). This massive user attrition severely harms the platform's financial viability: the final step-wise revenue under the Baseline condition collapses to a mean of $\$121.64$ ($\text{SD} = \$4.54$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [\$120.04, \$123.21]$).

In stark contrast, activating the bridging algorithm (\textbf{Bridge Only} condition) almost entirely halts user churn. Under this condition, the cumulative churn rate drops precipitously to a mere $0.38\%$ ($\text{SD} = 0.30\%$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.27\%, 0.49\%]$) at step $200$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:churn}). This drop in user flight is statistically robust and represents an exceptionally large effect size: Mann-Whitney $U = 900.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} < 1.5 \times 10^{-9}$, Cohen's $d = 23.97$. Consequently, the platform retains nearly its entire user base, maintaining an active user count of $498.1$ agents ($\text{SD} = 1.52$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [497.57, 498.63]$).

This near-perfect retention is driven by a massive reduction in behavioral frustration, which falls to a mean of $0.5819$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0576$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.5617, 0.6024]$, Mann-Whitney $U = 871.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} < 3.1 \times 10^{-8}$, Cohen's $d = 3.08$). Relieved of toxic conflict, the platform's final step-wise revenue is successfully preserved, reaching a mean of $\$199.24$ ($\text{SD} = \$0.61$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [\$199.03, \$199.45]$, Mann-Whitney $U = 0.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} < 1.5 \times 10^{-9}$, Cohen's $d = -23.97$). Financially and operationally, the bridging algorithm functions exactly as designed: it successfully keeps users engaged, dampens frustration, and maximizes advertising throughput.

\subsubsection{The Paradoxical Ideological Backlash}
However, an inspection of the ideological landscape reveals the paradox. Rather than pacifying the network, the suppression of backfire interactions leads to a statistically significant \textit{increase} in global ideological polarization. In the Baseline condition, the final-step polarization $\sigma$ settled at a mean of $0.9550$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0389$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.9409, 0.9683]$). Under the Bridge Only condition, final-step polarization rises to $0.9904$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0042$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.9888, 0.9918]$), representing a large and highly significant increase (Mann-Whitney $U = 135.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} < 0.0002$, Cohen's $d = -1.28$; see Figure~\ref{fig:polarization}).

This counterintuitive outcome arises from a structural selection effect. In the Baseline condition, highly extreme and entrenched agents frequently interact with their ideological opponents, experience unmitigated backfire cascades, accumulate high levels of frustration, and ultimately churn. Their permanent exit from the network mathematically truncates the distribution's extremes, artificially lower-bounding global polarization. 

By contrast, the bridging algorithm shields these highly extreme, entrenched users from frustration, allowing them to remain active indefinitely. As these extreme agents remain on the platform, they continue to pull their locally connected neighbors toward the ideological poles, preventing any consensus from forming. By suppressing the behavioral symptoms of polarization (exit and frustration), the bridging algorithm preserves the extreme voices, ultimately locking the entire social system into an ideological deadlock.

\subsection{Trust Segregation: The Relational Cost of Social Judgments}
When we enable the co-evolution of dyadic trust (\textbf{Trust Only} condition), we observe a dramatic structural transformation of the relational network. In this condition, trust is not a static parameter but a dynamic link attribute that co-evolves with dialogue. 

In the Baseline condition, trust is not modeled and remains fixed at a nominal value of $0.5$ across all edges, yielding a flat trust landscape where ingroup and outgroup trust are identical, and the trust segregation ratio is exactly $1.00$. 

However, under the Trust Only condition, the co-evolution of opinions and trust leads to a stark polarization of relationships (see Figure~\ref{fig:trust_seg}). Ingroup trust (trust between agents who share the same opinion pole) climbs to a near-perfect mean of $0.9814$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0055$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.9794, 0.9832]$). Conversely, outgroup trust (trust between agents with opposite opinion poles) completely collapses, dropping to a mean of $0.0415$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0256$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.0331, 0.0511]$).

This extreme divergence in relational capital manifests in a massive surge in the network's Trust Segregation Ratio, which reaches a mean of $35.61$ ($\text{SD} = 31.50$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [26.29, 47.64]$). Compared to the Baseline, this represents an exceptionally large effect size: Mann-Whitney $U = 0.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} < 7.3 \times 10^{-11}$, Cohen's $d = -1.55$. 

This relational division highlights the dual role of trust. Within ideological camps, trust functions as a powerful constructive glue: high ingroup trust increases the perceived influence weight $w_{ij}$, promoting consensus and smoothing over minor internal disagreements (the ``friends can argue'' buffer). 

Across ideological camps, however, the asymmetry of trust co-evolution ($\delta_- / \delta_+ = 2.5$) acts as a destructive solvent. The rapid relational penalty incurred during backfire events outpaces the slow build-up of trust during agreement, leading to a rapid erosion of cross-cutting ties. Combined with passive decay ($\lambda = 0.001$), cross-camp trust is systematically eradicated, leaving the network structurally segregated into two highly cohesive but mutually hostile relational silos.

\subsection{Moderation Analysis: The Bridge \texorpdfstring{$\times$}{x} Trust Interaction}
To test the central theoretical contribution of our study---whether the presence of dyadic trust moderates the systemic impact of the bridging algorithm---we executed a seed-paired interaction analysis. Because our batch runs are paired by random seed, we can compute the paired differences in final metrics to isolate how the bridging algorithm's effect changes in the presence of co-evolving trust:
\begin{align}
    \Delta_{\text{alone}} &= \text{Metric}(\text{Bridge Only}) - \text{Metric}(\text{Baseline}) \\
    \Delta_{\text{with trust}} &= \text{Metric}(\text{Full Model}) - \text{Metric}(\text{Trust Only})
\end{align}
Comparing these two distributions of differences using Mann-Whitney $U$ tests reveals a series of highly significant and theoretically critical Bridge $\times$ Trust interaction effects (see Table~\ref{tab:interaction}).

\paragraph{Active Users, Frustration, and Churn:} The interaction effects on physical user retention metrics (Active Users, Churn Rate, and Avg. Frustration) and financial performance (Revenue) are statistically non-significant after Bonferroni correction ($p_{\text{adjusted}} = 1.0000$). The bridging algorithm retains its powerful physical and economic protective effects regardless of whether trust is enabled: it consistently rescues approximately $190$ to $194$ users from churning, decreases frustration by $\approx 0.39$, and preserves $\approx \$76.00$ in step-wise revenue.

\paragraph{Outgroup Trust and Trust Segregation:} Crucially, we find a highly significant interaction effect on relational variables. The bridging algorithm's effect on \textbf{Outgroup Trust} is significantly moderated by trust co-evolution (Interaction Cohen's $d = -0.8219$, Mann-Whitney $U = 660.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} = 0.0093$). While the bridging algorithm has a nominal effect of $0.0$ on outgroup trust in the absence of a trust system, when trust is enabled, the addition of the bridging algorithm (\textbf{Full Model} condition) \textit{significantly drives outgroup trust even lower}, decreasing it by an average of $-0.0127$ (dropping from a mean of $0.0415$ in the Trust Only condition to a final mean of $0.0288$, $\text{SD} = 0.0108$, in the Full Model).

Correspondingly, we find a highly significant interaction effect on the \textbf{Trust Segregation Ratio} (Mann-Whitney $U = 240.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} = 0.0093$). When combined with trust co-evolution, the bridging algorithm further \textit{intensifies} relational segregation, increasing the Trust Segregation Ratio by an additional $+1.9854$ (from a mean of $35.61$ in the Trust Only condition to a final mean of $37.5992$, $\text{SD} = 11.47$, in the Full Model).

This is a key empirical result. By suppressing cross-cutting, negative-affect interactions, the bridging algorithm successfully prevents the behavioral explosion of frustration and churn. However, this algorithmic intervention does not build mutual tolerance. Because the algorithm intercepts conflict and replaces it with silence (Zone 2, non-commitment), agents are deprived of the opportunity to engage in constructive debate, learn from disagreements, or rebuild trust. Instead, they are left in a state of mutual isolation. Over time, the lack of active, constructive contact, combined with passive relationship decay ($\lambda = 0.001$), slowly and relentlessly erodes whatever fragile cross-ideological trust remains, leading to a relational network that is even more deeply segregated than one without any moderation at all.

\subsection{Echo Chambers: Emergent Structural Clustering}
To analyze how these cognitive and relational dynamics reshape the topological communication patterns of the platform, we observe the \textbf{Opinion-Weighted Clustering Coefficient} (see Figure~\ref{fig:clustering}). This metric quantifies the local density of triadic relationships where all three connected agents share the same opinion pole, serving as a direct measure of structural echo chambers.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig7_echo_chambers.png}
    \caption{Emergent structural echo chambers, measured via the opinion-weighted clustering coefficient over time. The Bridging Algorithm (Bridge Only and Full Model) significantly reduces local triadic homogeneity compared to the Baseline, although global polarization remains extreme. Error ribbons show $95\%$ confidence intervals.}
    \label{fig:clustering}
\end{figure}

In the Baseline condition, the final mean opinion-weighted clustering coefficient is $0.1040$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0209$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.0968, 0.1115]$). When the trust co-evolution system is enabled alone (\textbf{Trust Only}), structural clustering remains statistically unchanged at a mean of $0.1050$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0237$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.0968, 0.1134]$, Mann-Whitney $U = 420.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} = 1.0000$, Cohen's $d = -0.05$). This indicates that trust co-evolution on its own, despite severely segregating relationships, does not change the physical clustering of opinions beyond the baseline scale-free structure.

However, when the bridging algorithm is activated, we observe a highly significant and substantial decrease in structural clustering. In the \textbf{Bridge Only} condition, opinion-weighted clustering drops to a mean of $0.0829$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0152$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.0774, 0.0881]$), representing a significant decrease relative to the Baseline (Mann-Whitney $U = 721.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} = 0.0038$, Cohen's $d = 1.16$). 

Similarly, in the \textbf{Full Model} condition (Bridge ON, Trust ON), the opinion-weighted clustering coefficient settles at a mean of $0.0866$ ($\text{SD} = 0.0137$, $95\%\ \text{CI} = [0.0816, 0.0914]$), which is also a significant reduction compared to the Baseline (Mann-Whitney $U = 681.0$, $p_{\text{adjusted}} = 0.0393$, Cohen's $d = 0.98$).

This structural shift occurs because the bridging algorithm, by intercepting and neutralizing extreme backfires, dampens the rate at which agents are repelled toward absolute ideological extremes. While the global polarization remains extremely high (due to the retention of extreme users who would have otherwise churned), the local clusters are slightly less uniform because agents are spared the extreme repulsive forces that drive absolute ideological homogeneity in tight neighborhoods. 

However, this lower clustering does not represent a cure for polarization. Rather, it represents an artificial preservation of a fragile, ideologically deadlocked, and relationally hollowed-out network: a system where agents remain physically connected in triangles, but where their interpersonal trust is strictly partitioned, and all cross-cutting channels are completely dry.
